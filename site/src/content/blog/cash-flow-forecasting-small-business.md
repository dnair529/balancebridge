Here's a sentence that stops most business owners cold the first time they hear it: **profitable businesses run out of money all the time.**

It sounds impossible. If you're making money, how can you not *have* money? But ask anyone who's run a contracting company waiting 60 days on a big receivable while payroll hits every other Friday. Or a restaurant owner staring down a slow February after a December that looked great on paper. Profit and cash are related — but they are not the same thing, and confusing them is one of the most common ways good businesses get into serious trouble.

The fix isn't complicated. It's a habit called cash flow forecasting, and the gold-standard version for small businesses — the 13-week cash flow forecast — is something you can genuinely build in a spreadsheet this week. Here's why it matters and exactly how to do it.

## Why Profit Is Not Cash

Your Profit & Loss statement measures whether your business model works. Your bank account measures whether you can make payroll Friday. Those are different questions, and they diverge for very ordinary reasons:

- **Timing of receivables.** You invoice a client $40,000 in March. Your P&L says you earned $40,000 in March. Your bank account says nothing happened, because the client pays in May.
- **Timing of payables.** You buy $15,000 of materials on terms. The cash leaves later than the expense appears — or earlier, if you had to prepay.
- **Things that eat cash but never touch the P&L.** Loan principal payments, owner draws, equipment purchases, and inventory buildups all drain the bank account without showing up as "expenses" on your income statement.
- **Things that hit the P&L but not cash.** Depreciation reduces profit without a dollar leaving the building.

Put together: you can post your most profitable quarter ever and still bounce a rent check, because profit lives on paper and payroll lives in your checking account.

## What a 13-Week Cash Flow Forecast Is

A 13-week cash flow forecast is a simple rolling schedule that answers one question, week by week, for the next quarter: **how much cash will actually be in the bank?**

Why 13 weeks? It's one quarter — long enough to see problems while you can still fix them, short enough to forecast with real accuracy. Weekly granularity is the key. Monthly forecasts hide the danger: a month can "average out" fine while week two of that month leaves you $9,000 short on a payroll Friday.

The structure is dead simple:

**Starting cash + cash in − cash out = ending cash.** Each week's ending cash becomes the next week's starting cash. That's the whole model.

## How to Build One, Step by Step

### Step 1: Start With Real Bank Balances

Add up today's balances across your operating accounts. Not what QuickBooks *thinks* — what the bank says, adjusted for any checks or payments that haven't cleared yet.

### Step 2: Forecast Cash In (Be Pessimistic)

Week by week, list money you actually expect to *receive* — not revenue you'll earn:

- Open invoices, slotted by when each customer *actually* pays (if they're always 15 days late, forecast them 15 days late)
- Predictable recurring revenue (subscriptions, retainers, average card deposits)
- Expected new sales, discounted for realism

Golden rule: forecast receipts by *deposit date*, not invoice date, and when unsure, push cash in a week later than you hope.

### Step 3: Forecast Cash Out (Be Complete)

This is where forecasts fail — not because owners are bad at guessing, but because they forget the lumpy stuff. Comb through the last three months of bank statements and capture:

- **Payroll and payroll taxes** (the most important line — mark those weeks)
- Rent, utilities, insurance, software subscriptions
- Vendor bills and inventory/material purchases
- Loan payments — *including principal*
- Credit card payments
- Quarterly items: estimated taxes, insurance premiums, sales tax remittances
- Owner draws (be honest)

### Step 4: Do the Math and Find the Danger Weeks

Compute ending cash for each of the 13 weeks. You're looking for two things: any week where the number goes negative (a crisis), and any week where it dips below your comfort floor — for most small businesses, a sensible floor is a few weeks of operating expenses.

### Step 5: Update It Weekly (This Is the Whole Trick)

Every week — pick a day, keep the appointment — replace forecasts with actuals, roll the window forward a week, and adjust. A 13-week forecast that isn't updated is a screenshot; updated weekly, it's radar. The update takes maybe 20–30 minutes once the template exists.

## A Simple Worked Example

Here's an illustrative forecast for a fictional small contractor — let's call it a plumbing company with eight employees. Numbers are for example only, and abbreviated to four weeks to show the mechanics:

| | Week 1 | Week 2 | Week 3 | Week 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Starting cash** | $32,000 | $44,500 | $16,500 | $34,000 |
| Customer payments | $28,000 | $6,000 | $38,000 | $9,000 |
| **Total cash in** | $28,000 | $6,000 | $38,000 | $9,000 |
| Payroll + payroll taxes | — | $26,000 | — | $26,000 |
| Materials/vendors | $9,000 | $4,000 | $12,000 | $5,000 |
| Rent + insurance | $4,500 | — | $2,500 | — |
| Loan payment | $2,000 | — | — | — |
| Truck repair (one-off) | — | $4,000 | — | — |
| Owner draw | — | — | $6,000 | — |
| **Total cash out** | $15,500 | $34,000 | $20,500 | $31,000 |
| **Ending cash** | **$44,500** | **$16,500** | **$34,000** | **$12,000** |

Look at what this reveals. The month as a whole is fine — cash in ($81,000) roughly matches cash out ($101,000 against a healthy starting balance). But **Week 2 ends at $16,500** and **Week 4 ends at $12,000** — both payroll weeks, both uncomfortably close to the edge for a company with a $26,000 payroll. One slow-paying customer in Week 3 and this business misses payroll in Week 4.

A monthly forecast would never have shown that. The weekly view did — with weeks of warning. And warning is everything, because the fixes are easy early and brutal late:

- **Pull cash in sooner:** call the two biggest open invoices today, offer a small early-pay discount, invoice progress billings faster, take deposits on new jobs.
- **Push cash out later:** ask key vendors for terms, shift the owner draw a few weeks, delay the non-urgent purchase.
- **Bridge the gap:** draw on a line of credit *before* the crunch. Banks lend umbrellas on sunny days; a forecast tells you to ask while it's still sunny.

## Tools: Spreadsheet, Software, or Done-for-You

**A spreadsheet is genuinely fine** — most 13-week forecasts in the real world live in Excel or Google Sheets. Thirteen columns, your cash categories as rows, and the discipline to update it weekly beat fancy software you ignore.

**Dedicated forecasting tools** (there are several good ones that sync with QuickBooks Online) earn their keep when you want automatic actuals, scenario toggles ("what if we hire in September?"), and less manual entry.

**Done-for-you** means your bookkeeping or advisory firm builds and maintains the forecast as part of monthly service. This is a core piece of what CFO-level advisory looks like at the small business level — and it only works if the underlying books are current, which brings us to the last point.

## The Habits That Make Forecasting Actually Work

1. **Keep the books current.** A forecast built on unreconciled accounts is guesswork with formatting. Weekly-updated books are the raw material.
2. **Same day, every week.** Monday morning cash review, 30 minutes, non-negotiable.
3. **Track forecast vs. actual.** Your misses teach you your business's real rhythms — which customers lag, which months bite.
4. **Keep a cash buffer target.** Pick your floor (for example, one month of operating expenses to start, building toward three) and treat dips below it as action triggers, not observations.
5. **Forecast before you commit.** New hire, new truck, new lease? Drop it into the 13-week model first and watch what it does to the danger weeks.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How is a cash flow forecast different from a budget?

A budget says what you *plan* to earn and spend, usually monthly, usually for a year. A cash flow forecast predicts what will actually move through your bank account, weekly, near-term. Budgets set intentions; forecasts prevent surprises. Healthy businesses use both.

### How accurate does a 13-week forecast need to be?

The near weeks should be quite accurate (you already know payroll and rent); weeks 8–13 are educated estimates that sharpen as they approach. Perfection isn't the goal — early warning is. A forecast that's 85% right three weeks out will still save you from nearly every cash crisis.

### What if my business is seasonal?

Then you need this more than anyone. Seasonal businesses (landscaping, retail, tourism-adjacent restaurants) live and die by whether peak-season cash is deliberately parked to cover trough-season burn. A rolling 13-week forecast, plus a simple annual seasonality view, shows exactly how much of the good months must be saved for the lean ones.

### Can my bookkeeper build this for me?

Building the forecast needs current, reconciled books — that's the bookkeeping layer — plus someone to assemble and maintain the model, which is controller/CFO-level work.
