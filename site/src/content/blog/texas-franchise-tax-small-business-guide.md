Texas famously has no personal income tax, which is one reason so many businesses set up shop here. But "no income tax" doesn't mean "no state tax on businesses." Enter the Texas franchise tax — a tax that confuses nearly every new business owner, partly because of its name (it has nothing to do with owning a franchise) and partly because most small businesses end up owing zero but *still have filing obligations*.

## What Is the Texas Franchise Tax?

The franchise tax is Texas's version of a business tax. Officially, it's a "privilege tax" — a tax on the privilege of doing business in Texas. Despite the name, it applies to ordinary LLCs, corporations, and other entities, not just franchise businesses like fast-food chains.

Unlike an income tax, the franchise tax is calculated on a business's **margin** — a figure based on revenue with certain allowed subtractions — rather than on net profit. More on that below.

## Who Has to Deal With the Franchise Tax?

In general, the franchise tax applies to **taxable entities formed in Texas or doing business in Texas**, including:

- Limited liability companies (LLCs), including single-member LLCs
- Corporations (both C corps and S corps)
- Limited partnerships (LPs) and limited liability partnerships (LLPs)
- Professional associations and business trusts

Generally **not** subject to the franchise tax:

- Sole proprietorships (no formal entity — just you doing business)
- Most general partnerships owned entirely by individual people

Notice the trap: forming an LLC — which thousands of Texas businesses do for liability protection — brings you into the franchise tax system, even if you're a one-person shop with modest revenue. Many owners don't realize this until a notice from the Comptroller shows up.

## The No-Tax-Due Threshold: Why Most Small Businesses Owe $0

Here's the part that surprises people in a good way: **most Texas small businesses owe no franchise tax at all**, because Texas sets a revenue threshold below which no tax is due.

The threshold is $2.47 million in annualized total revenue for recent report years — but verify the current figure with the Texas Comptroller, as it's adjusted periodically. If your total revenue is at or below the threshold, you owe zero franchise tax.

### The 2024 Filing Change That Made Life Easier

Starting with 2024 reports, Texas made a welcome change: entities under the no-tax-due threshold generally **no longer need to file the No Tax Due Report** that used to be required every year. Before this change, thousands of small businesses that owed nothing still had to file a report annually — and got penalized for forgetting.

However — and this matters — being under the threshold doesn't necessarily mean zero paperwork. Many entities still have an information report obligation (a Public Information Report for LLCs and corporations, or an Ownership Information Report for certain other entities). Rules differ by entity type, so confirm your specific filing obligations with the Comptroller or your tax advisor.

The takeaway: "I owe nothing" and "I have nothing to file" are two different statements. Make sure you know which one applies to you each year.

## How the Franchise Tax Is Calculated (High-Level)

If your revenue is above the threshold, the tax is based on your **taxable margin**. Texas lets you calculate margin several different ways — and you generally get to use whichever produces the lowest tax:

| Margin Method | How It Works (Simplified) | Often Favors |
|---|---|---|
| Total revenue × 70% | Margin is capped at 70% of total revenue | Businesses with low costs and low payroll |
| Total revenue − cost of goods sold (COGS) | Subtract eligible COGS from revenue | Contractors, manufacturers, retailers, restaurants — businesses with significant direct costs |
| Total revenue − compensation | Subtract eligible wages and benefits | Service businesses with big payrolls (medical practices, professional services) |
| Total revenue − $1 million | Flat subtraction | Businesses modestly above the threshold |

After computing margin, the figure is apportioned to Texas (based on the share of your business done in Texas) and multiplied by the tax rate. The rate varies by business type — retailers and wholesalers generally pay a lower rate than other businesses. There's also an "EZ computation" option for businesses under a certain revenue level that trades a simpler calculation for a slightly different rate structure.

You don't need to memorize any of this. What you *do* need to know: the right method can meaningfully change what you owe, and choosing it requires accurate numbers for revenue, cost of goods sold, and compensation — which is precisely what clean bookkeeping gives you. Your CPA makes the method call; your books make the call possible.

## Key Deadlines: Circle May 15

The Texas franchise tax report is due **May 15** each year (or the next business day if the 15th falls on a weekend or holiday). A few things worth knowing:

- **Extensions are available**, but an extension to file is not an extension to pay — if you expect to owe, payment rules still apply. Your tax advisor can walk you through extension mechanics.
- **Missing the deadline has teeth.** Penalties and interest apply to late filings and payments, and a business that ignores its obligations long enough can lose its right to transact business in Texas — meaning, among other things, it can lose the ability to sue in Texas courts and its liability protection can be jeopardized. That's a steep price for a missed form.
- **New entities** get some runway: your first franchise tax report generally isn't due until the year after the year you formed. But put it on the calendar the day you form the entity, because "we didn't know" doesn't waive penalties.

## How Clean Books Make Franchise Tax Season Painless

Here's where we admit our bias — and then defend it. The franchise tax is, at its core, a bookkeeping-driven filing. Every input comes straight from your financial records:

- **Total revenue** comes from your P&L — which is only right if income isn't double-counted (hello, Undeposited Funds problems) or missing.
- **COGS** is only usable as a deduction method if your chart of accounts actually separates direct costs from overhead. If everything is lumped into "Expenses," your CPA can't cleanly run the COGS method — and you may pay more than you should.
- **Compensation** figures need to tie to payroll records.
- **Knowing whether you're under the threshold at all** requires trusting your revenue number in the first place.

## A Quick Compliance Checklist for Texas Entity Owners

1. **Know your entity type** and whether it's subject to franchise tax (most LLCs and corporations are).
2. **Confirm your annual filing obligations** — even below the threshold, an information report may still be required.
3. **Calendar May 15** every year, starting the year after formation.
4. **Keep revenue, COGS, and payroll cleanly separated** in your books all year.
5. **Watch the threshold** as you grow — crossing it changes your filing from a formality into a real calculation.
6. **Verify current thresholds and rates** with the Texas Comptroller annually; figures adjust over time.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I owe Texas franchise tax if my LLC made very little money?

If your annualized total revenue is at or below the no-tax-due threshold ($2.47 million for recent report years — verify the current figure with the Texas Comptroller), you owe no franchise tax. You may still have an information report to file, depending on your entity type.

### Is the Texas franchise tax the same as an income tax?

No. It's calculated on margin — revenue minus certain allowed subtractions — rather than net profit. A business can be unprofitable and still technically have a margin, which is one reason the calculation deserves professional attention once you're over the threshold.

### What happens if I never filed franchise tax reports for my LLC?

The Comptroller can assess penalties and interest, and prolonged non-compliance can lead to forfeiture of your entity's right to do business in Texas — which can put your personal liability protection at risk. If you're behind, don't panic; talk to a CPA about getting current, and get your bookkeeping caught up so the past-due reports can be prepared accurately.
