At some point, every growing business owner has this moment: you know you need financial help, you start looking, and you immediately drown in titles. Bookkeeper. Accountant. CPA. Controller. CFO. Fractional CFO. Are these different jobs? Different price tags? Do you need one of them, or all of them?

They are different jobs — genuinely different — and hiring the wrong one is one of the most common (and expensive) mistakes small businesses make. Hire a CFO when you need a bookkeeper and you'll pay strategy prices for data entry. Hire a bookkeeper when you need a CFO and you'll have beautiful records and no idea what to do with them.

Here's the plain-English breakdown.

## The Simplest Way to Think About It

- A **bookkeeper** records the past.
- An **accountant** interprets the past and keeps you compliant.
- A **CFO** plans the future.

Everything else is detail. But the detail matters when you're writing checks, so let's go role by role.

## What a Bookkeeper Does

The bookkeeper is the foundation of your entire financial system. Their job is making sure every dollar in and out of the business is recorded accurately and on time:

- Categorizing transactions in QuickBooks or similar software
- Reconciling bank accounts, credit cards, and payment processors every month
- Managing invoices and bills (accounts receivable and accounts payable)
- Supporting payroll processing
- Producing monthly financial statements — Profit & Loss, Balance Sheet, and ideally a cash flow report

**What it typically costs:** Outsourced bookkeeping for a Texas small business generally runs $300–$2,500 per month depending on complexity, with most established small businesses landing between $500 and $1,200. A full-time in-house bookkeeper typically costs $45,000–$60,000+ per year in salary alone.

**The catch:** everything downstream depends on this layer. If the bookkeeping is wrong, your accountant is analyzing fiction and your CFO is forecasting a fantasy. This is why "my cousin does it when she has time" is the most expensive free service in business.

## What an Accountant Does

An accountant works one level up from the transactions. Where the bookkeeper records, the accountant interprets, adjusts, and ensures the picture is technically correct:

- Reviewing and adjusting the books (accruals, depreciation, corrections)
- Preparing formal financial statements
- Tax planning and — if they're a CPA or enrolled agent — preparing and filing tax returns
- Advising on entity structure, major purchases, and compliance questions

A note on the term **CPA**: a Certified Public Accountant is an accountant with a state license, extensive exam requirements, and the authority to do things like represent you before the IRS and perform audits. All CPAs are accountants; not all accountants are CPAs. For tax filing, you'll generally work with a CPA or enrolled agent.

**What it typically costs:** Most small businesses engage a CPA seasonally or hourly. Business tax return preparation commonly runs from several hundred to a few thousand dollars depending on complexity, and advisory work is typically billed hourly at rates from roughly $150 to $400+.

## What a Controller Does (The Role Nobody Talks About)

Between bookkeeper and CFO sits the controller — the most underrated role in small business finance. A controller owns the *accuracy and process* of your financial operations:

- Reviewing the bookkeeper's work and closing the books each month
- Building internal controls so errors and fraud get caught
- Managing budgets vs. actuals
- Owning the financial calendar: closes, filings, renewals, deadlines

Think of the controller as quality control plus process management. Growing businesses often need controller-level oversight years before they need CFO-level strategy.

## What a CFO Does

A Chief Financial Officer looks forward. They take the clean historical data everyone else produced and turn it into decisions:

- Cash flow forecasting and scenario planning ("Can we survive losing our biggest client?")
- Pricing and margin strategy
- Hiring plans, compensation modeling, and unit economics
- Financing: preparing for loans, lines of credit, or investors — and negotiating them
- Long-range planning: new locations, acquisitions, eventual exit

**What it typically costs:** A full-time CFO commands a significant six-figure salary — typically $200,000+ in total compensation — which is why almost no small business hires one. Fractional CFO services (more below) typically run from around $1,500 to $7,500+ per month depending on scope.

## The Signals You Need Each Role

**You need a bookkeeper (or a better one) if:**

- You're doing your own books at 10 p.m. and hating your life
- Your accounts haven't been reconciled in months
- Tax time means shoeboxes, panic, or an annoyed CPA
- You can't produce a current P&L within a few minutes of being asked

**You need an accountant/CPA if:**

- You have any tax filing obligation (so: everyone)
- You're choosing or changing entity structure
- You're facing a notice from the IRS or the Texas Comptroller
- You want proactive tax planning instead of April surprises

**You need controller-level support if:**

- The books are done, but late, and month-end drags on for weeks
- Multiple people touch the finances and nobody reviews anything
- Budgets exist but nobody compares them to reality
- You've been burned by an error nobody caught for months

**You need CFO-level support if:**

- You're making six-figure decisions (hires, locations, equipment, loans) on instinct
- You're profitable on paper but perpetually cash-poor and don't know why
- A bank or investor asked for projections and you had nothing to send
- You're thinking about selling the business in the next few years and want it valuation-ready

## How Fractional Roles Work (And Why They Changed the Game)

A decade ago, this hierarchy was mostly out of reach: small businesses got a bookkeeper and a tax-time CPA, and everything in between was the owner's gut. The fractional model changed that.

**Fractional** simply means you buy a slice of a senior professional's time — a set scope of work each month rather than a full-time salary. A fractional controller might close your books and run your budget review for a fixed monthly fee. A fractional CFO might deliver a rolling 13-week cash forecast, a quarterly strategy session, and on-call support for big decisions.

The math is compelling: for example, a business might get genuine CFO guidance for $2,500 a month — $30,000 a year — instead of a $250,000 full-time package. You get the expertise without the overhead, and you can scale the engagement up or down as the business changes.

The best structure for most growing businesses is a **stack**: outsourced bookkeeping as the foundation, controller oversight layered on as complexity grows, fractional CFO guidance added when decisions get big, and a CPA relationship for tax filing throughout. One firm handling the first three layers — and coordinating cleanly with the fourth — keeps everyone working from the same numbers.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can one person be my bookkeeper, accountant, and CFO?

At very small scale, one skilled professional can wear multiple hats. But the roles pull in different directions — detail work vs. strategy — and as you grow, you'll get better results (and better checks and balances) from a layered approach, whether that's a team at one firm or a few specialists who coordinate.

### Do I need a CPA if I have a bookkeeper?

Yes, at minimum for tax filing. A bookkeeper keeps your records accurate; a CPA (or enrolled agent) prepares and files returns and provides tax strategy. They work best as a team — clean books from the bookkeeper make the CPA faster, cheaper, and more effective. Balance Bridge coordinates with your CPA directly so nothing falls between the two.

### What's the difference between a controller and a CFO?

A controller looks after accuracy and process — closing the books, enforcing controls, comparing budget to actuals. A CFO looks after strategy — forecasting, financing, and big-decision support. Controllers perfect the rearview mirror; CFOs work the windshield.

### At what revenue level do I need a fractional CFO?

There's no magic number, but many businesses start benefiting somewhere between $1M and $5M in revenue — or earlier if they're capital-intensive, growing fast, or preparing for financing. The better trigger isn't revenue; it's the moment your decisions carry more risk than your gut can price.
